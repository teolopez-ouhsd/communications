What 487 people told us about our websites

Students, parents, and staff answered short surveys about finding things on the district and school sites. This page shows what they said, where they agree, and what it means for how we build the new sites.

One dot is one person who answered

StudentParentStaff Ring = answered the Spanish survey

These are the people who chose to answer. We did not have totals for each group, so read this as who spoke up, not as every student, family, or staff member.

The short version

Five things everyone kept saying

1

The basics take too long

Calendar and bell schedule are what most people come for, and they are the things people say they cannot find quickly.

2

Old content costs trust

Blank pages, old staff lists, and calendars that are not current came up in every group, without being asked.

3

Menus and search send people the wrong way

Too many paths, unclear labels, and a search that returns the wrong page. Many people start with Google instead.

4

Parents are on phones

Most parents use a smartphone and prefer texts. Spanish-speaking families also want translation and phone calls.

5

Some problems are not website problems

Unanswered phones, who keeps pages fresh, and how families learn about ParentSquare need people and policies, not a new design.

Where they disagree

Where does it live?

English-survey parents are nearly split on enrollment (26 district, 32 school) and lean district on board agendas (36 to 20). Spanish-survey parents lean toward the school site for both. Staff split on IT support (29 to 31). These pages need links from both sites.

More content, or less clutter?

Students ask for less on the page and the important things first. Staff ask for more: course descriptions, forms, tutoring, program explanations. A short main menu with deeper pages behind it serves both.

Phone, or laptop?

82% of English-survey parents and 90% of Spanish-survey parents use a phone. 72% of English-survey students use a school laptop. Design phone-first for families, and make it work well on a Chromebook.

The platform

Almost no one mentioned the platform itself. One student asked us to keep the current one and fix accessibility, and one staff member looked forward to the new one. People care about finding things, not which tool builds the page.

What to build

What this means for the structure of the new sites

These are proposals tied to the evidence above. The menu labels should be tested with a card sort and a tree test before they are locked.

Main menu for the district site

Put these a click from the homepage

    Utility bar and footer

    Search on every page, an easy-to-see translate option on phones, calendar, ParentVue, StudentVue, ParentSquare, staff login, contact, accessibility statement.

    School sites mirror the district

    Same labels

    Families, Students, Staff, Athletics and Activities, Academics, Calendar, Contact. People learn it once.

    Same required blocks

    Bell schedule, calendar, staff directory, portals, lunch menu, bus, campus map, athletics clearance packet, and a phone number that is answered.

    Linked, not copied

    District and school forms point to each other. One calendar source with school filters, one forms library.

    Merge, cut, rewrite

    Merge

    • School and district calendars into one source
    • All forms into one searchable library

    Cut

    • Empty pages
    • Pages that only forward to another site
    • Old club and staff pages until verified

    Rewrite

    • Unclear menu labels, like a 20-item staff menu
    • Page titles, so "menu" finds lunch
    • Wordy pages

    What a redesign cannot fix by itself

    The new sites can link to these, but people have to own them.

    • Calls and emails that go unanswered
    • Who keeps each page current, and how often
    • Old staff names on lists and pages after people leave
    • How families first learn about ParentSquare and the portals
    • Systems the site links to, like grades and the IT ticket system
    Request vs. root problem

    A request is a clue, not a work order

    People tell us what they want in the form of a fix: "put it on the menu," "add a QR code," "make the search better." Those requests are clues about a problem. Our job is to find the problem behind them, then pick the best fix. The best fix may be different from the one someone named, and sometimes it is not a website change at all.

    For webmasters: when you get an ask, write the root problem down first. If you cannot say what the person was trying to do, you are not ready to build anything yet.

    Five questions before building what someone asked for

    1

    What were they trying to do?

    The task, not the tool.

    2

    Who else hits this?

    How many people, and which groups?

    3

    How bad is it?

    Could not finish, or mildly annoying?

    4

    What kind of fix?

    Design, content, process, or policy.

    5

    How will we know?

    A test or a number we can repeat.

    Using the data

    How this data should guide decisions

    Use the surveys to find patterns and set priorities. Do not use them to pick labels, layouts, or colors. Those need tests.

    What it is good for

    • Spotting problems that repeat across groups
    • Building the starting list of top tasks
    • Seeing which pages need links from both district and school sites
    • Giving us a "before" number for a repeat survey

    What it cannot do

    • Speak for everyone. People chose to answer, and one school gave about half of the student answers
    • Rank what matters most. Comment counts show what people noticed
    • Show behavior. Use site analytics and search terms for that
    • Choose labels or layouts

    Four things to weigh for any item

    Reach

    How many people, and how many groups, raised it?

    Pain

    Does it block a task, or is it a mild annoyance?

    Fix

    Can design or content solve it, or does it need a decision?

    Proof

    Several groups and both languages, or one comment?

    Use it at every stage of the redesign
